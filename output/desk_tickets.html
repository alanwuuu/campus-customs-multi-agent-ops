Reflection: how well did the Campus Customs team work?

Evidence comes from the clean run: 5 agent runs, 54 MCP tool calls, 147,567 tokens, and 3 human approvals. Cash went $3,400 → $152, and all three tickets ended resolved (output/resolved_tickets.json, output/audit_trail.json).

1. How well the agents handled each ticket

Ticket 101 · Bulldog tee Good
  • What went right: Inventory spotted that Bulldog Print Co was blocked by overdue invoice 501. Accounting queued the invoice, and the PO was correctly held until the invoice was paid. The customer got honest "estimated, not confirmed" wording for 2026-09-05.
  • What went wrong: invoice 501's description is "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S". The shop had already ordered this tee, so paying the invoice should release it. The team still queued a new 1-tee PO, and we approved $8 we probably didn't need to spend. The fact that settled it was in get_ticket_context, which Boss read, but the PO decision was made two hand-offs later by Accounting, working from Inventory's summary.
  • Messy outbox: two customer drafts (#1, then #2), because there's no tool to update a draft.
Ticket 102 · Rent Strong
  • Routing: exactly the planned route, Boss → Facilities → Accounting. The lease was confirmed against the database ($2,400, due 09-02, not overdue). The payment was affordable ($2,552 ≥ $2,400) and approved, and the lease rolled forward to 10-02.
  • Scope: Accounting stayed within the ticket and didn't re-queue invoice 501. That was the scope bug we fixed in Problem 5.
  • Weak spot: landlord draft #3 says the rent was "queued … for human approval". That's internal process language. The Customer Service prompt forbids it, but the Facilities prompt doesn't. Draft #4 replaced it, so the landlord now has two drafts.
Ticket 103 · Bulk hoodies Strong Costly
  • The best decision of the run: 8 of 20 in stock, and the $264 restock was more than the $152 left after tickets 101 and 102. So the team didn't queue a PO the register would have refused. It compared 5%, 10% and 15% with quote_discount and offered 5% on the 8 available ($440.80, about a 60% margin). The draft makes no promise about the other 12.
  • Cost: it was the most expensive run (24 model calls, 19 tool calls, 52,777 tokens). Accounting was invoked twice, once by Inventory and once by Boss. Its second session re-ran get_ticket_context, get_cash_position and check_stock_and_restock because it doesn't remember the first.
  • Gap: nothing holds the 8 hoodies for the club, so another order could take them.

Why it worked: the shop rules are enforced in the tools, not just the prompts. Amounts come from the database, POs to blocked vendors can't be approved, and payments over the balance are refused. So even where judgment was imperfect (the $8 PO), nothing broke a rule. Cash never went below $152, and every payment row has a human approved_by.

Routing was predictable; money and judgment were not. Every routing prediction held, because Boss's prompt routes by ticket ownership and the specialists' tool access is narrow. The misses all involved judgment: the extra PO, the discount size, and what counts as resolved. They also all trace back to things our plan didn't model. 3. Where delegation worked well

  • Inventory → Accounting on a blocked vendor (101, 103). Inventory can see the block but can't touch money, so it handed the vendor, PO cost and blocking invoice id to Accounting in one self-contained task. That's how the overdue invoice got paid before the PO, which is the shop rule in action.
  • Facilities → Accounting for rent (102). Facilities checked the lease and Accounting checked cash, and only Accounting can call request_payment. The split works as a safety boundary: the agent that reads lease data can't move money.
  • Boss → Accounting for the discount (103). Keeping the pricing question separate from stock let Accounting weigh three discount levels against the $152 cash reality. Boss then made the business call (5%) instead of the numbers agent.
  • Boss only called the agents it needed. Across 5 runs no ticket used all five agents. Facilities never touched a product ticket, and Customer Service never touched rent.

4. Where multiple agents added unnecessary complexity

  • Re-reading the same ticket. get_ticket_context was called 17 times in 5 runs, once per agent session. Every hand-off starts the agent cold, which is a big part of why 93% of tokens were input (138,101 in vs 9,466 out).
  • Duplicate Accounting work in 103. Two separate Accounting sessions each pulled cash and stock again: get_cash_position ×2 and check_stock_and_restock ×2, on top of Inventory's own check.
  • Losing information between hand-offs (101). Boss saw the invoice description, Inventory summarized it, and Accounting acted on the summary. The detail that the invoice was the tee reprint didn't survive two hand-offs, so we got the redundant $8 PO.
  • Follow-up runs used the whole team for bookkeeping. 101's second run spent 24,283 tokens, and Inventory re-checked stock just to repeat an ETA we already had. 102's second run used 13,714 tokens to confirm "approved" and write one landlord note.
  • Duplicate drafts. Drafts #1/#2 and #3/#4 exist because agents can only create drafts, not revise them.

5. What one agent with tools could have handled

  • All of ticket 102. It needs get_ticket_context → request_payment(rent, 1) → create_draft, which is three tool calls with no judgment. One agent with those tools would have used about 3 model calls instead of 12 + 7 across two runs.
  • Closing tickets after approval. "All requests decided and a final draft saved, so resolved" can be checked in code on the backend right after the human approves. That would remove the follow-up runs for 101 and 102, about 38k tokens.
  • Ticket 101's diagnosis. Stock, vendor block and invoice description are all in one get_ticket_context result. A single agent seeing them together would likely have paid invoice 501 and skipped the extra PO.
  • Customer and landlord drafts. These are templated summaries of facts the deciding agent already has. A create_draft tool on Boss, with Customer Service's tone rules, would save a hand-off per ticket.
  • Where multiple agents still made sense: 103, the only ticket with a real trade-off between stock, cash and pricing. It also matters when we want the separation of duties: only Accounting can request money.

7. Three Campus Customs problems our current team can't solve

Closing the Yale AI Club sale

The club accepts the 5% offer and pays $440.80. We can't record a sale, take money in, hold or remove the 8 hoodies from inventory, or receive the tee shipment when 09-05 arrives. Cash only goes out, stock never changes, and the desk date never moves forward.

A return or refund

Tauhid receives the size S tee and wants to swap it, or a customer asks for a refund. There's no orders table, no customer payment history, and no refund or return-to-stock tool. Accounting can only pay vendor invoices, rent and POs.

Shop maintenance or a lease change

The Chapel Street storefront has a leak, or the landlord proposes a new rent at renewal. Facilities can read the lease and write drafts, but it can't change lease terms, and vendors only lists merchandise suppliers and a courier. There are no repair contractors and no work orders.

Bottom line

The team was safe and correct on the rules: all 7 rule checks passed, $152 matches cash_accounts, and no message was sent. It was right on routing, with 3 out of 3 first contacts as predicted. But it was heavier than the tickets needed. Only ticket 103 really benefited from several specialists. Ticket 102 and both follow-up runs would have been cheaper and just as safe as a single tool-using agent plus a backend "auto-resolve" check. The one costly judgment miss, the $8 duplicate tee order, came from information lost in a hand-off, which is the main risk of splitting one ticket across agents.
